What's Actually Changed

Real, sourced updates on Medicaid, Medicare, and disability services — not rumors, not spin

Living tracker Last updated August 2026

Policy changes fast right – especially right now. Much of what circulates about Medicaid, Medicare, and disability services is half-true, outdated, or panic-driven by the time it reaches a group chat. We're here to slow that down — every entry below is checked against a primary or established source, dated, and updated as things change.

claim "Medicaid work requirements are already in effect everywhere."
reality Not yet, nationally. The federal work requirement — 80 hours a month of work, school, or volunteeringBenefitsUSA, June 2026 for able-bodied adults in the ACA expansion group — was signed into law July 4, 2025, but most states don't have to enforce it until January 1, 2027. A few states, like Nebraska, are starting earlier. If you're a parent or caretaker of a child under 14, or a person with a disability, you are exempt from this requirement entirely.
Last checked: August 2026
claim "Nobody knows how the new Medicaid rules will actually work yet — even the states don't know."
reality This one's closer to true than most rumors. CMS didn't release its interim final rule until June 3, 2026 — just seven months before the first implementation deadline — which left states scrambling to redesign eligibility systems, renewal forms, and notices on a compressed timeline. If your state's process feels confusing or inconsistent right now, that's a fair read of the situation, not something you're misunderstanding.
Last checked: August 2026
claim "Medicaid cuts don't really affect people with disabilities — there are exemptions."
reality Direct work-requirement exemptions exist, but the financial pressure is broader than the exemption category. The 2025 reconciliation law is projected to cut federal Medicaid spending by roughly $911 billion over 10 years. When federal funding drops, the cost burden shifts to states — which can mean tighter state budgets for the HCBS waivers and disability services that were never guaranteed entitlements to begin with. Exemption from the work requirement doesn't mean insulation from every downstream effect.
Last checked: August 2026
claim "My Medicare Advantage plan got worse this year because of something I did wrong."
reality Almost certainly not you. Major insurers — UnitedHealth, Humana, CVS/Aetna — all pulled back Medicare Advantage offerings for 2026, citing federal reimbursement cuts, in many cases shrinking provider networks or exiting counties entirely. About 2.6 million people had their specific plan terminated for 2026 — more than double the prior year. If your coverage changed or your network shrank, it's very likely part of this pattern, not an individual error.
Last checked: August 2026

Beyond Medicaid: changes at HHS itself

Some of the biggest changes affecting disability, mental health, and autism services aren't in Medicaid at all — they're happening inside the Department of Health and Human Services (HHS). A few of these are worth tracking separately, since they move on a different timeline (budget proposals vs. signed law) and it matters which stage each one is actually at.

claim "HHS is being completely dismantled — nothing is left."
reality Overstated, but the real version is still significant and can feel bleak. HHS has been “reorganized” rather than eliminated — a new “Administration for a Healthy America” (AHA) now consolidates the Health Resources and Services Administration, the Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration, and parts of the CDC under one structure. Separately, the Administration for Community Living has been proposed for dismantling, with its functions redistributed elsewhere — ACL is the agency that has historically overseen HCBS and independent-living programs, so this one is worth watching closely if you rely on those services.
Last checked: August 2026 — reorganization is real; full scope of ACL's absorption elsewhere is still developing
claim "Autism research funding has been cut."
reality True, based on the numbers currently available. NIH autism research funding dropped from about $147 million to roughly $116 million, alongside a broader ~26% cut to HHS research budgets. A separate leaked internal budget proposal would go further, eliminating the CDC's dedicated autism public-health programming entirely and shifting oversight to the new AHA structure — that piece is a proposal, not yet finalized, so it's worth watching rather than treating as settled.
Last checked: August 2026 — funding cut is confirmed; full CDC program elimination is still a proposal
claim "The federal budget cuts to HHS are just proposals — nothing has actually changed yet."
reality Partly true, and worth being precise about. The FY2026 HHS budget cut (roughly 25%, about $32 billion) was a proposal defended in congressional testimony, not something families need to react to as already-finalized law — federal budgets go through Congress and can change significantly before taking effect. That said, some actions don't require a budget vote: agency reorganizations, grant terminations, and specific program eliminations can happen administratively. The termination of specific children's health grants covering autism early identification and adolescent mental health is one example of a cut that already happened, separate from the larger budget proposal.
Last checked: August 2026 — distinguish proposed budget cuts (not yet law) from administrative actions (already in effect)

Special education and food assistance

Two more changes worth understanding on their own — one about who administers your kid's IEP rights, one about grocery budgets.

claim "My kid's IEP doesn't matter anymore since they're closing the Department of Education."
reality Your child's legal rights are unchanged — what has moved is who administers them. On June 16, 2026, the Department of Education announced it's shifting day-to-day administration of special education (IDEA) to HHS, and civil rights enforcement to the Department of Justice. Officials have stated the agreement does not alter IDEA's guarantee of a free, appropriate public education — the underlying law is unchanged, and statutory responsibility technically remains with the Education Department. That said, disability advocates including The Arc and COPAA have raised real concerns that running special education through a health agency risks treating disability as a diagnosis to manage rather than a child to teach. Your IEP itself doesn't change. Which federal office you'd contact with a complaint, potentially does.
Last checked: August 2026 — administrative transfer confirmed; full implementation details still emerging
claim "SNAP eligibility changes probably haven't affected very many people yet."
reality It's already substantial. More than 4 million people lost SNAP access between July 2025 and April 2026 — an 11% national drop, with children among those most affected. The work-requirement age range expanded, and exemptions were removed for veterans, homeless individuals, former foster youth, and parents whose children are 14 or older — parents of kids under 14 remain exempt from the work requirement. States are also now covering a larger share of administrative costs, which several state agencies have said may mean slower processing and more errors going forward.
Last checked: August 2026 — eligibility changes are active law, not proposals

Health insurance and vaccines

Two more that show up constantly in group chats — one about marketplace insurance costs, one about vaccine coverage. Both are genuinely more nuanced than a single headline captures.

claim "My ACA marketplace premium went up because I did something wrong on my application."
reality Not you — this is a confirmed, already-in-effect change, not a rumor. Enhanced ACA premium tax credits expired at the end of 2025, and premium payments for subsidized marketplace enrollees rose by an average of 114% in 2026 as a direct result — from about $888/year to roughly $1,904/year for the same coverage. This wasn't a proposal that stalled; it already happened and is reflected in your actual 2026 bill. If your premium jumped, this is almost certainly why.
Last checked: August 2026 — this change is already in effect, not pending
claim "The new CDC vaccine schedule means my kid's vaccines aren't covered by insurance anymore."
reality More complicated than a yes or no. HHS released a revised childhood immunization schedule on January 5, 2026, cutting universal recommendations from 18 diseases to 11 — but that revised schedule is not currently in effect due to an ongoing federal court order, so it hasn't actually changed coverage requirements yet. Separately, major insurers, through their trade association, have committed to covering all vaccines that were ACIP-recommended as of September 1, 2025, at no cost through the end of 2026, regardless of the schedule dispute. School entry requirements are set by your state, not the federal government, and haven't changed based on this alone. Check with your specific plan if you're unsure, but there's no confirmed nationwide coverage loss right now.
Last checked: August 2026 — revised schedule blocked in court; insurer coverage commitments currently hold through 2026
