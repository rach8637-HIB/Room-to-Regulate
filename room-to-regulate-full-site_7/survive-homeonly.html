He's an Angel Everywhere Except With Me

5 min readEssay

Every parent-teacher conference starts the same way, and every time, it takes me a second to place the kid they're describing. Helpful. Follows directions. Kind to the other kids. Takes correction well. I nod along, and some small, tired part of me wants to ask if we're talking about the right child, because the version of him that walks through our front door most afternoons is not the version anyone else has ever met.

At home, the same request can take five tries. Not because he doesn't hear me — he hears me the first time, I'd bet money on it — but because somewhere along the way, without either of us deciding it on purpose, the real rule became: nothing actually has to happen until we get to the version of me that means it. He's not being sneaky about it. He's just learned, the way kids learn everything, through repetition: request, ignore, repeat, argue, explain, consequence. Actual compliance lives at step five, so step five is where he shows up.

It would be so much easier if he was just like this everywhere. Then I could point to him as the explanation and let myself off the hook a little. But he isn't like this everywhere, and that's the detail that keeps me up some nights, because it means the hardest version of him isn't a character trait — it's specifically about here, about us, about whatever it is that makes home the place he finally puts the effort down.

He holds himself together everywhere else because everywhere else feels like it requires holding together. Home is the one place he's decided is safe enough to stop performing.

I used to take that personally, in the worst way — like I was doing something wrong that made him worse instead of better. Now I think it might be closer to the opposite. That's not nothing. That might even be something like trust, in its most exhausting possible form.

It doesn't make the fifth request less tiring. But it's changed what I think I'm actually looking at. Not defiance for its own sake. Not a character flaw showing up selectively. Just a kid who's spent all day being good at being good, and has nothing left to spend on doing it twice.

I'm trying to build a home where the first ask matters as much as the fifth — not because he doesn't deserve the safest place to fall apart a little, but because I want that safety to include structure, not just softness. I haven't figured out how to get us there yet. But I've stopped needing him to be the angel at home too. I just want him to know home is safe, and safety doesn't mean nothing counts here.
