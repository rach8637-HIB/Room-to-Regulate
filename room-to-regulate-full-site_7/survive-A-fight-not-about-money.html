The Fight That Isn't Actually About Money

4 min readEssay

It starts, every time, with numbers. A charge on the account. A therapy copay stacked on another therapy copay. An activity fee for the third extracurricular this season, because if he's not moving, he's spiraling, and stillness costs us more than the sign-up fee ever will.

"We agreed we were cutting back." He's not wrong. We did agree. I meant it when I said it, the same complete way I mean most things in the moment I say them — and then the moment passes, and so does the plan, and I don't know how to explain that the agreement didn't fail because I didn't care. It failed because by the time the receipt was in my hand, the agreement wasn't in the room with me anymore.

That's the part that's hardest to say out loud: I don't always see the spending while it's happening. Not the big stuff — I see that. The accumulation. The forty small yeses that individually made complete sense and collectively became a number neither of us saw coming, because no single moment where it happened felt like a decision. It felt like keeping a kid regulated. It felt like ordering dinner because I hadn't had a spare thirty seconds to plan one. It felt like solving whatever was on fire in front of me, with the fastest tool in reach, which was almost always choosing time – a moment of intended calm – over money.

He sees a pattern. I see forty separate five-alarm fires, each one fully justified on its own.

We're both right, which is the worst kind of right to be in an argument.

The fight that follows isn't really about the dollar amount. It's about trust — his, that the plan we agreed to will hold; mine, that I'm not actually as careless as the running total makes me look. And underneath both of those is something neither of us says out loud: that the real cost isn't on the statement at all. It's the pull each of us feels to be right – the need for validation we're not getting through life's daily churn. It's the fight this always turns into, which costs more than either of us can afford, paid in a currency the bank doesn't track.
