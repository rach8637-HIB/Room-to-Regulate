Native Partner Card — Component & Framework

Reference doc for how sponsored content will look, where it can appear, and where it never can

Internal reference

This page documents how brand partnerships should look and behave on Room to Regulate — the actual HTML/CSS component, where it's allowed to appear, where it's permanently off-limits, and the disclosure language required to stay both legally compliant and trustworthy to this specific audience.

The component itself

Styled to match the site's existing resource cards exactly — same white background, same border, same corner radius — so a partner placement reads as "another resource," not an interruption. The only visual difference is the mauve "Partner" label and a disclosure line at the bottom. Below is a live example, exactly as it would appear at the end of a Solve piece.

Where this card is allowed to appear

Only at the natural end of a Solve piece, after the free content and template — never inside the essay itself, never interrupting a Survive piece, and never inside a step-by-step script where it could be mistaken for part of the instructions. One partner card per page, maximum.

Where this card is never allowed — no exceptions

  • "What's Actually Changed" — the fact-check tracker stays permanently ad-free. A sponsor on a page fact-checking government policy is a credibility risk this site can't take.
  • "Verified Resources" — same reasoning. This page's entire value is that every listed resource was chosen because it's trustworthy, not because it paid. Mixing sponsor placements into that list, even clearly labeled, undermines the page's reason to exist.
  • Any Survive essay — these are catharsis pieces. A sponsor placement inside or immediately after raw, personal content reads as exploiting someone's hardest moment for revenue. Survive pieces can link to Solve pieces (which may carry a partner card), but never carry one directly.
  • The Glossary — reference content stays neutral by design.

Partner vetting standards — before anyone is approached

Given this audience is often financially stressed, emotionally raw, and actively researching mental health and disability topics, the bar has to be higher than typical content-site advertising standards:

  • No payday loans, buy-now-pay-later services, debt consolidation, or any credit product marketed around financial desperation
  • No supplement, "natural cure," or unproven wellness product making health claims — especially anything adjacent to autism/ADHD "cure" framing
  • No multi-level marketing companies, regardless of category
  • Health/therapy partners must be real, licensed, insurance-integrated services — not direct-to-consumer products making clinical-sounding claims without oversight
  • Every partner must be something the site would credibly recommend even if unpaid — the paid relationship should never be the only reason a product appears

Disclosure requirements (FTC compliance)

The label must say "Sponsored" or "Paid Partnership" explicitly — not vaguer terms like "Promoted" or "Recommended" alone, which don't meet FTC clarity standards for material connections. The disclosure line at the bottom of the card is required on every placement, not optional styling.
