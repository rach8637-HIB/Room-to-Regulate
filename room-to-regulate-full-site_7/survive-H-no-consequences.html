There Are No Consequences That Work

5 min readEssay

There are no consequences that work. I know this the way you know something you've relearned a hundred times and still haven't let yourself fully believe, because believing it means admitting the tool I keep reaching for was never actually built for this job.

I see summer the same way I did as a kid. Beautiful sunshine. Freedom, fun, every possibility still open and none of them cancelled out yet. I feel it through the window near my desk — that specific golden slant of light that used to mean school's out — and something in me still races toward it the way it did when I was seven, except now the racing looks like trying to close out four more emails before pickup, because if I can just finish fast enough, today can be the good kind of day. I'll pick them up early. We'll do karaoke tonight. They can ride bikes in the driveway. Maybe there's enough time for the pool. At least a family dinner, all of us, together. I build the whole afternoon in my head before I've even left my desk, gold-lit and easy, exactly the summer I remember having.

I pick up one kid. Sometimes that part goes fine — just the two of us, no friction yet, still inside the version of the day I planned. Then we get the other one, and by the time both car doors are shut, they're already arguing. I don't know who started it. I don't know what was even said. I just know, instantly, with total certainty, that the afternoon I built this morning is already gone, and we haven't even left the parking lot.

By the time we pull into the driveway, I'm yelling. It takes about five minutes. Five minutes of me holding my breath, trying to out-wait the noise, and then I'm not holding anything anymore.

I march them toward their chores like a drill sergeant, and something in me locks onto one single objective — get it done, get it done fast, get it done now — because if I can't have the golden afternoon, I will at least have order. I cannot deviate from it. There's no room in me left for anything except the list and the speed at which it gets finished.

More fighting. More yelling. More consequences, stacked on top of consequences, until I've taken away everything I have left to take, and it still isn't landing the way it's supposed to.

And then — after all of it, after the yelling and the marching and the losses — he asks for more. Screen time, a snack, permission to go back outside. Asks like none of what just happened is connected to what he's asking for now. Like the consequence I handed him twenty minutes ago simply isn't in the room with us anymore.

I stare at him, genuinely bewildered, and the question that shows up every single time is the same one: Am I too hard on them? Is this all my fault?

I don't have a clean answer. I know the sunshine still gets me every single summer, the same way it got me as a kid, promising a version of the day that was never actually available to us once two dysregulated kids got in a car together. I know the consequences I hand out in the heat of it don't work, not because I'm not harsh enough, but because by the time I'm handing them out, we're already past the part of the day where anyone in that car could have received them. I just don't yet know what to build instead of the golden afternoon I keep, every summer, still expecting to have.

If this hit close

If you've handed out a consequence that vanished the second it left your mouth — the problem probably isn't the consequence. It's where in the day you're reaching for it.
