I Know Exactly What That Feels Like, Which Is Why I Can't Watch It Happen to Him

5 min readEssay

The teacher says it kindly. She means it kindly. "He's clearly so bright — he just needs to focus." She says it like it's a small thing, a footnote, the kind of comment that closes a parent-teacher conference on a hopeful note.

I have heard that sentence before. Not about him. About me. Different classroom, different decade, same exact shape: so smart, but. So smart, but can't sit still. So smart, but forgets homework. So smart, but talks too much, too fast, too off-topic, in a way that made teachers and other kids label that child as "a lot". A kid who became, privately, terrified of ever being seen as lazy, even for one single second since everyone kept saying that laziness was the only remaining explanation.

Nobody used the word ADHD on me. This was a long time ago, and I was, inconveniently, a girl, and my hyperactivity had migrated inward into a brain that wouldn't stop rather than a body that couldn't. So instead of an evaluation, I got "so smart, but" for a decade, said by well-meaning adults who thought they were praising me, and I quietly built an entire identity around the "but."

I know exactly what happens next if nobody intervenes. I lived the next fifteen years of it.

Now I'm in a different chair, in a different building, listening to a different teacher say a version of the same sentence about my son — and something old and specific comes roaring back up, uninvited. I know what it does to a kid to be told, year after year, that the gap between their potential and their output is a character problem — that "so smart" is a compliment they haven't earned the right to yet, because the "but" keeps getting in the way.

I sit in that meeting translating in real time: not just what the teacher is saying about him, but what an eight-year-old version of me would have heard underneath it. You are only as good as your worst subject. Your effort is visible. Your struggle is not.

I want to stand up and say: he is being asked to perform executive function his brain doesn't reliably produce, in a system built for kids whose brains do, and being quietly blamed for the mismatch. I want to say: I know this story. I have lived inside it. Please do not hand him the version of it that took me thirty years to unlearn.

I don't say all of that. I say some of it, carefully, in the measured voice I've had to build specifically for these meetings — because if I say it the way it actually feels, I become the parent they write off as "difficult," and that helps no one, least of all him.

But I leave that meeting differently than I walked in. Because now I know something I didn't fully let myself know before: I'm not just advocating for my son in that room. I'm rewriting my own narrative, one meeting at a time.

If this hit close

If you recognized your own "so smart, but" in this — the system that failed to name it for us often still fails to name it for our kids, just with better paperwork.
