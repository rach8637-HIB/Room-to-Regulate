It's Not That Nobody Wants to Work Anymore

We're being asked to parent like we don't have jobs, and work like we don't have kids

5 min readPolicy explainer

The labor force participation rate dropped to 61.5% this summer (source: Fortune, July 2026) — the lowest reading outside of the pandemic in fifty years. The headlines that followed split roughly into two camps: economists pointing to boomer retirements and immigration policy as the real drivers of a shrinking labor supply, and a louder, older narrative resurfacing underneath it — that people, especially younger workers, just don't want to work anymore.

Both explanations miss something that shows up constantly in conversations among working parents, and rarely in the data write-ups: the labor force participation rate for mothers sits at 73.9%, compared to 93.7% for fathers (source: U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2025 data) — a nearly 20-point gap that has nothing to do with ambition and everything to do with who's still expected to hold the household together while also holding down a job.

The collision nobody's naming

Four separate forces have stacked on top of each other over the last few years, each one reasonable in isolation, brutal in combination.

Remote and hybrid work, which quietly filled the childcare gap for years by letting a parent be present without technically being "off," has been rolled back hard. Return-to-office policies trickled in slowly, then arrived in force. Meanwhile, nothing replaced the flexibility that disappeared — no expansion of affordable childcare, no shift in school schedules to match a full workday, nothing structural to fill the exact gap RTO reopened.

At the same time, schools are asking more of parents than ever before. And companies, simultaneously, are asking more from the employees they've kept, while reducing headcount, often citing AI-driven efficiency gains that haven't yet actually reduced anyone's workload — they've just reduced the number of people available to share it.

Parent like you don't have a job, work like you don't have kids — at the exact moment the informal supports that used to make that barely survivable have also been quietly shrinking.

The boomer-childcare assumption, checked against the actual data

One explanation that comes up is that grandparents are absorbing the gap — stepping in for childcare as they also step back from the workforce themselves. After all, the largest generation is the one retiring. The data says close to the opposite: the number of grandparents providing childcare to grandchildren with a parent present in the household dropped by 29% over the past decade (source: ASA Generations, University of Pittsburgh NCFS analysis). Whatever is filling the space RTO reopened, it isn't a wave of newly available grandparents.

Why this isn't a motivation problem

None of this is about willingness to work. The participation data, read honestly, describes a supply-and-demand mismatch layered directly on top of a caregiving-infrastructure gap that was never rebuilt after the pandemic exposed exactly how load-bearing it had been. Calling it laziness isn't just unkind — it's a wrong diagnosis, and a wrong diagnosis guarantees a wrong fix.

One thing worth doing with this

This isn't a single-bill fix — it's closer to the mental-load infrastructure gap, just visible now at the scale of the entire labor market instead of one household. The same senators worth contacting about paid leave are worth hearing this framing from too: parents aren't opting out. The support structure was quietly pulled out from under a system that was depending on it.
