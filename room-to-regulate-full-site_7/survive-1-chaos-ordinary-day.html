What Looks Like Chaos on an Ordinary Day

12 min read Essay

Alarm goes off. It's 5:30am. Just a few more minutes. I calculate the seconds it takes to check each item off the list. Maybe, just maybe, I'll get it all done with more than five and a half hours of sleep. My eyes shut. Shit — it's 6:00.

I wash my face, brush my teeth, calculating, calculating. I move to the kitchen. Three things stopping me — three things I asked to be put away last night. Deep breath. I clean them up. Oh no — I forgot to make breakfast last night. I forgot the frozen water bottles. Shit. Shit. Shit. It'll be fine. I feed the dogs. I finish setting out everyone's everything. Another deep breath.

I enter the sunroom with a sigh of relief. I stretch. I start my to-do list for the day. My day doesn't look too crazy, I think. I have amazing ideas for everything, and today I'm putting them into action. Maybe I'll even have enough time to sit with a cup of coffee, nothing on my mind, for two whole minutes. Sitting down for two minutes would be amazing.

The dogs start barking. The howling rips through my peaceful thoughts like a tornado on a bright, sunny day. I leave the sunroom — twice — begging them to stop. Begging that no door opens upstairs. Focus, I plead with myself. I check the clock. Shit — I'll never have time to run. Deep breath. I sink into five minutes of yoga instead.

I grab a leash and walk the dogs, my mind racing. Calculating time with what-ifs. If I wash my face in the shower, will that save enough time to sit for a moment? Do I need to wash my hair today — could I not? Is my medicine ready to go? If I'd done that ahead of time, I could've saved thirty seconds. By the time the dogs have taken their sweet time, ripped me through the streets, and I've picked up two rounds of poop with the same hand holding the leash — terrified it's going to fly in my face if either of them looks back — I have five minutes to get ready.

All that hope for two peaceful minutes feels like a lifeline snipped mid-air. Like standing at the edge of a cliff with no turning back.

I shove the feeling down. Okay. I can do this. I'll just be in a good mood. I don't have to be completely ready. I need to ask for help when I need it. He'll help. It'll be fine.

I enter the room. He's still sleeping. It takes three tries to open my morning medicine, because no — I did not do that the night before. I ask if he can help get things moving, since I'm running late. "I can try," he says, "but I have to be in the city today." I bite my lip, take a breath, get in the shower.

When I get out, the kids' alarms are going off. The dogs are leaping and yelping on the bed. He's still sleeping. And that's it. I can't take anything more. I start shaking. I start screaming. I'm hateful and blameful. I feel nauseous — I was racing so fast I forgot to eat before taking my vitamins again. This should be fun.

As I take a breath in, there's a knock on the door. "Can I come in?" The plan, for months, had been to be dressed and ready, sitting calmly, arms open for that first embrace of the day — to ward off the anxiety I create in her. The chaos that surrounds her. I ask her to wait. Before I know it, she's screaming. Crying. Because her brother heard me yell, heard the escalation in my voice, and unleashed his own onto her. She used to glow with excitement at the start of each day. I watch her shrink, and it kills me. With every unpredictable moment in this house, she grows smaller, quieter, dimmer.

And as those heartbroken thoughts seep through me — I scream. I hate every second of it. It all feels broken.

Where is he? Oh, right. Bathroom. I check the mirror, start brushing my hair, and the fighting starts downstairs. Before I know it, someone has hit someone. Both kids are in my room, and the dogs bark and bark and bark. A wild cacophony until I finally yell, "GET OUT."

Downstairs, I take a breath. Before I can let it out: "You said I'd get your breakfast from now on." Before I can apologize: "Why don't I have a water bottle? You said you were handling it. Weren't you doing laundry yesterday too?" I snap and tell them both to eat in silence.

How do I fix this? I think. We need more fun. I check Amazon. I end up with stress toys and a new rewards system. Annoyance creeps in. We end up with so many systems because I'm the only one who stays consistent with any of them. I push the thought down, smile, kiss him goodbye. "Five minutes. In the car in five minutes."

I leave the room. I come back two minutes later. They've stopped fighting — but neither has touched breakfast. Nothing's in the backpack. No sunscreen, despite both visual and audio reminders. "What are you doing? We have three minutes to get in the car!" She starts sobbing — "I'm sorry, I'm sorry" — still not moving. He acts like this is the first time today, or ever, he's heard such a thing. I'm racing, throwing things into the car with one hand.

I get them both dropped off and race home for a call I swore I'd have thirty minutes to prep for. I have four. I pull up materials and brain-dump my way through it. It doesn't go well.

It all feels impossible. I throw myself into research, determined to do better, to prove myself. As the confidence starts to build, a message flashes across the screen — a long, wordy, over-budget paragraph. I'll do better today, I swear to myself. More messages. More questions. More budget. I see the sliver of self-doubt ping against my mind's windshield and look away. But every notification lands like a jackhammer, drilling into my core.

I stare at my to-do list. It all seems so much harder. I stare at the clock. The day is waning. There's no way I get through most of this. Another breath. I check tomorrow's calendar — what can I push? Tomorrow is just today, one day closer to the next one.

What can I control? I get up. I walk the dogs. I see a half-finished project and pick it up. An hour passes. Shit — groceries and the store that actually carries what she needs. I check my phone: a missed call from my dad, one from my mom, twenty-four messages across two group chats and one friend. I skim mostly to clear the notifications, moving anything urgent onto the list. I owe that friend a response — I physically cannot do it right now, that's an hour I don't have. Focus. Ninety minutes left and so much to do.

Groceries and the store. There's nothing in the house and no time to stop, so — delivery, and he's going to be annoyed. How does every day end up like this? It's either this or the kids starve. Fine. Dinner: check. I didn't actually make it, but I figured it out, so that counts.

Kid stuff. I call a friend to ask for help. "No? No, it's okay, I'll figure it out." Maybe a sitter. Maybe a neighbor — no, that got weird last time. A text comes through: another delivery order, really? I thought we were cutting back. Of course. No sitter. I'll figure it out. I call him. "Will you be home early today? I have a thing, the kids have a thing, and no one else can help." "Just let me know when you're leaving."

I'll figure it out. I check the list again. I've accomplished nothing for work. He's annoyed about money, again. How do I stop the endless cycle of money arguments? Clearly I don't have time to leave the house for anything, so I just need to focus on the business and take on more clients. An hour until pickup, and a call in there too. I restructure the list — what can happen with kids around, what can't. I focus on the can't. One project, one call. Halfway satisfied with the project, I get on the call and freeze — no time to prep. I jumble my thoughts and hang up feeling shitty. How am I supposed to take on more clients when I can't manage what's already on my plate?

Breath. Eight texts. Three former coworkers asking for intros. A money text. A friend ignoring my ask for help and giving me her own rundown instead. Two friends reminding me of something I'd completely forgotten I said I'd handle. I sigh and get in the car.

I call my dad back. He's annoyed I haven't called enough, hasn't seen us enough, launches into a story about an event. I only have a few minutes before I need to call my mom too, so I tell him I have to go. He's clearly annoyed. I call my mom. No answer.

I'm last to pick up both kids, and neither is pleased. "Can we go to Rita's?" — interrupting his sister before so much as a hello. Now that's going to be a thing. Cue the fighting. I yell. I check off the rest of the list in my head. I race to the activity. Other parents look at me like I belong in a straitjacket — wrong shoes, no makeup, racing in last-minute with too many bags. My kids throw their stuff at me, quite literally, on top of me, as I yell, "I bought groceries. We're picking them up. We are not staying late." Famous last words.

He's stopping for a drink in the city between trains. I start calculating again. She needs a bath. I look up — her friend is here. I smile at the other mom while thinking, shit, now I'm never getting her out of here on time.

The kids reappear. "Can we eat here? Please? My friends are!" "No — home, like I said. We still need groceries." Forty-five minutes past the agreed time, I get them out. We get the groceries. In the car: "The dogs haven't been out or fed. It's late. We had fun, but I'm really going to need help when we get home." "Okay, mommy," in unison.

We get home. The dogs are frantic. "Grab your stuff." The phone rings — my mom. "Hey — guys, stop! Don't — !" "This seems like a bad time," she says. I say something snarky back. I'm tired, I'm hungry, there are dogs barking and children screaming. "Sorry I bothered you," she says, and we hang up.

I feel like crying. Every part of the day comes at me like a cannonball I can't stop, and I just snapped at my mom like I'm ten years old — because she's the one place that's supposed to feel safe. The guilt and self-doubt crash in, opening old wounds. My kids feel that way about me too. I'm supposed to be their safe place. I need to be more understanding. I breathe and vow to walk in doing just that.

I open the door. The dogs dive down the stairs. "Hey — can you help with the groceries, like we said?" I look around, my brain taking inventory, and the promise I made seconds ago melts under the panic. It's 7:00. The dogs haven't been fed. Both kids dumped their stuff on the floor. Groceries need to come in. She needs a bath. Dinner, still. And everything I didn't get to earlier.

I start rattling things off, pleading. "If we move fast, maybe we can go outside before bed." One breath later, he's perched on his chair with two snacks in hand: "No thanks." And I lose it. He yells back. She quietly helps — until bath time, when the battle begins with her instead. Almost an hour past bedtime, I realize I've answered no one and finished about half my work. I haven't eaten all day, and somehow that's the only thing that's getting done tonight.

I give the dogs their medicine, walk them in the yard. As I'm about to sit down, he walks in. One of two things happens: I word-vomit every adult thought I've had all day, which usually ends in a fight — having asked him nothing. Or he says, "Did you pick up my—" and I snap. Because when. Just — when.

At some point the conversation turns to money, and to feeling entirely incapable at the end of a long day, and I agree to something that feels like a loss of autonomy in the light of morning. But that's an argument for tomorrow, or the day after.

Whichever day it becomes, it will look like chaos on an ordinary day.
