I Learned to Keep the Peace by Staying Quiet. Parenting Needs Something I Was Never Taught.

5 min readEssay

I've been highly sensitive for as long as I can remember being anything at all. Feelings arrived in me at full volume, other people's included, and somewhere very early I learned the rule that kept the peace: don't say the angry things. Swallow it. Smile through it if you can manage the smile. The version of me that made it through childhood, through friendships, through every room I've ever been in, is a version fluent in one specific skill — going quiet instead of going off.

Nobody warned me that parenting doesn't run on that skill. Parenting runs on something closer to its opposite. Kids don't feel safe in the absence of conflict — they feel safe inside structure, inside rules that hold even when they push on them, inside a parent who can say no and mean it without either dissolving into silence or detonating. Kids need someone to hold the line. My whole nervous system was trained to do the other thing: disappear the line rather than defend it.

So here's what actually happens, most days: I disappear the line for as long as I possibly can. I let things slide that shouldn't slide. I say nothing when something in me is already lit up and screaming, because saying something has always felt like the dangerous option. And then — because the line still needed holding, because kids still need the no even when their mother doesn't know how to give it calmly — it finally comes out anyway. Except by the time it comes out, it doesn't come out as a boundary. It comes out as an explosion. All the swallowed conflict from every smaller moment I let slide detonates at once, aimed at whichever request finally became the one I couldn't quietly absorb.

I am, somehow, both too permissive and too dysregulated, and I don't think those are two separate problems. I think they're one problem wearing two faces.

At night, once the house is finally quiet, the guilt takes its shift. I lie there running the math on what I might be building into my kids without meaning to — wondering if this exact pattern, the swallow-then-detonate cycle, is exactly how you hand a child their own future rejection sensitivity, their own conviction that other people's calm is conditional and can flip without warning. I worry I'm not managing my own dysregulation. I worry I'm manufacturing theirs.

Last night my son told me I don't show him enough love.

I don't think that's entirely true. I also don't think he's wrong. I think what he's actually naming, in the only language an eight-year-old has for it, is the gap between the mother who goes quiet to keep the peace and the mother who finally speaks and sounds like a storm — and how neither one, in the moment, feels like being loved out loud. Quiet reads as distant. Explosion reads as danger. Somewhere in between is the thing he actually needs, and most days I still don't know how to live there.

I don't have the ending yet. I know the pattern now, which is more than I knew a year ago. I know it's not a character flaw so much as a skill I was never taught, layered on top of a nervous system that learned the wrong lesson early and learned it well. Some nights that's enough to fall asleep on. Some nights it isn't, and I just lie there, replaying his voice, trying to figure out how to be a mother who can hold a line without either going silent or catching fire.
