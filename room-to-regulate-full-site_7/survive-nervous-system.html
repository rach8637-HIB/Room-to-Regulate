When Her Meltdown Becomes My Emergency

4 min readEssay

There's a specific moment — I can feel it starting before I've consciously registered why — where her crying stops being her crying and becomes something happening to me. My chest goes tight first. Then my hands. Then a kind of ringing focus that isn't calm, it just looks like calm from the outside, the way a held breath looks like stillness.

With my first, I used to think this was just what good parenting felt like under pressure — the alertness, the readiness, the way I could snap into problem-solving mode instantly, no lag time. I called it being "on it." It took a second kid, and a few hundred more data points, to notice that "on it" and "overwhelmed" were sharing the same body, and I'd never actually learned to tell them apart.

Here's what it looks like from inside: she's five, and she's sobbing about something that is, by any adult measure, small — the wrong cup, a snack she didn't ask for, a plan that changed by ten minutes. And some days, I can meet that with exactly the calm, appropriately-sized response it deserves. I can see you're really upset. I'm here with you. Other days, the same-sized meltdown lands in my body like an actual five-alarm emergency, and I am not responding to a five-year-old's disappointment anymore. I am responding to something else entirely, wearing her crying like a costume, and I don't get to choose which version of me shows up until I'm already standing in the middle of it.

Some days, my reaction to her isn't about her at all. It's an alarm system going off in response to something that happened hours before she even started crying.

It took me embarrassingly long to notice the pattern had almost nothing to do with the size of what she was upset about, and almost everything to do with what else had already used up my nervous system that day. On a full-tank day, her big feelings are just big feelings — loud, developmentally normal, nothing my body needs to treat as a threat. On an empty-tank day, the exact same crying reads as danger, and my body reacts accordingly, whether or not there's any actual danger in the room.

I don't have a clean fix for this yet. I know the difference matters — that a kid deserves a parent who can tell the difference between "she's having a hard five-year-old moment" and "I am currently flooded and reacting from somewhere old, not somewhere present." Some days I can catch it before it lands on her. Some days I can't, and I have to go back afterward and repair it, and tell her clearly that the size of my reaction was mine, not a fair measure of what she'd actually done.

I'm learning to ask myself the question before I ask it about her: is this actually an emergency, or is this just what's left of me by 4pm, wearing her crying like it's the reason.
