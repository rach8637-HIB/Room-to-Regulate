"You Just Don't Stick With Things" — and Other Stories That Might Not Be True

4 min readReflection tool

Adults with ADHD often carry a set of old explanations for their own childhood behavior — quit too many activities, never followed through, wasn't disciplined enough, didn't try hard enough. Most of those explanations were handed down by well-meaning adults working with the information and patience they had at the time. Some of them were accurate. Some of them were never actually about character at all — they were about unmet needs, unrecognized ADHD, or circumstances no one examined closely enough before assigning a story to them.

The problem isn't just that some of those old stories might be wrong. It's that unexamined stories about yourself quietly become the lens you parent through — the assumptions you make about your own kid's behavior, before you've actually looked closely at what's happening underneath it.

"I just wasn't that interested" vs. "I wanted to keep going, but the support I needed wasn't there, and no one looked closely enough to notice the difference." Those aren't the same story, even when they explain the exact same outcome.

Reflection questions

Not to relitigate childhood, but to separate signal from inherited story:

  • What's a behavior pattern from your own childhood that an adult explained with a character trait ("lazy," "too sensitive," "doesn't stick with things")? Is there another, equally plausible explanation for the same behavior that no one considered at the time?
  • When you notice your own kid doing something that echoes a trait you were criticized for — is your first instinct to correct the behavior, or to correct the story before you even look closely at what's happening?
  • Whose voice is loudest when you're frustrated with your child's behavior — your own honest read of the moment, or an old inherited script about what that kind of behavior "means" about a person?

Why this matters specifically for parenting effectively

A parent operating from an unexamined inherited story tends to respond to the label instead of the situation — reacting to "here we go again, being dramatic" instead of "what's actually happening for my kid right now." Kids can tell the difference, even when they can't articulate it.

A useful habit: pause before the label lands

Instead of: reacting instantly to a behavior with the conclusion already formed ("typical, he never follows through")
Try: "What's actually happening here, right now, for this kid, in this moment?" — asked literally, out loud if it helps, before any older story gets to answer the question first.
The takeaway

Not every explanation you were given as a kid was accurate, and not every explanation you're tempted to give your own kid will be either. The work is staying curious enough, in the moment, to tell the difference.
