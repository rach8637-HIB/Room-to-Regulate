Where Do All the Water Bottles Go? A Missing Persons Report

3 min readEssay (the fun kind)

Every school year starts the same way: a fresh multi-pack of water bottles, still in the shrink wrap, gleaming with possibility. This year, I tell myself, we have a system. Each kid has a color. Each color has a name written on it in Sharpie, then relabeled in paint pen when the Sharpie inevitably rubs off by day three. I feel, for one shining August morning, like a woman who has her life together.

By October, I own four bottles and none of them match a lid.

I don't know where they go. I want to be clear that I have looked. I have checked the backpack, the car, the second car, the bottom of the pool bag from July, the lost and found bin that the school insists is "regularly cleared" and yet somehow contains what I can only assume is a bottle from the Obama administration. I have looked under beds. I have looked in beds. I once found one, half-full of something I chose not to investigate, wedged between the couch cushions like it was hiding from a warrant.

Water bottles do not get lost. Water bottles ascend. They are called home to some higher plane of existence, some great water bottle beyond.

They leave behind only a faint impression on my bank account and a child standing in front of me at 7:42am saying, with total sincerity, "I don't know where my water bottle is," as though this is happening to both of us for the first time, as though we are both simply victims of an unknowable cosmic force, as though I did not personally purchase this exact bottle eleven days ago.

I have started buying them in packs of six like I'm provisioning a small expedition. I have accepted that the good bottle — the one with the strap, the one he actually likes, the one that doesn't leak — will vanish within 72 hours of purchase, while the bottle he hates, the free one from a work conference in 2019, will outlive us all, indestructible, eternal, always somehow back in the cabinet no matter how many times I try to donate it to a cause more deserving.

I don't have a system. I have a graveyard. I have a monthly Target run that exists for the sole purpose of water bottle resurrection. And every single time, standing in that aisle, restocking the fleet, I think the same thing: this time will be different.

It will not be different. But God, I respect the delusion. It's the only thing keeping me upright some mornings.
