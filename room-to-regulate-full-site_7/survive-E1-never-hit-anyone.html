He's Never Hit Anyone. He's the One Who Gets Hurt — and Doesn't Get Believed.

5 min readEssay

He has never hit another kid. Not outside our home anyway. I want to say that plainly, first, because the story that gets told about him — loud, big feelings, "a lot" — has a way of implying otherwise, and it isn't true. It's not true.

What's true is harder to say out loud because it makes me furious in a way I have to sit on: he's the one who gets hurt. Pushed, excluded, mimicked behind his back, and when he comes and tells me about it — voice too fast, the words tumbling out in the disorganized way they do when he's upset — I watch other adults' faces do a specific thing. A polite skepticism. A "well, he does tend to escalate things," said kindly, like it's a mitigating detail instead of the whole point.

He's impulsive. He's anxious. He talks fast and interrupts and sometimes gets the sequence of events wrong when he's upset, telling you the ending before the beginning, doubling back, correcting himself mid-sentence. And because of that — because of how he tells you something, not what he's telling you — adults quietly discount it.

Composure reads as credibility. Nobody says that part out loud, but every adult in that room is acting on it.

So the kids who exclude him, who've figured out exactly how to do it in the gaps adults aren't watching, have also figured out something else: that if he tells, he probably won't be believed, not fully, not the way a calmer, more organized kid would be. That's not a theory. I've watched it happen. I've sat in a classroom and watched a story he told accurately, if messily, get quietly downgraded to "he might be misunderstanding" while the kid who did it stood there, calm and articulate, and got the benefit of every doubt his composure bought him.

This is the loneliness I didn't have words for until it had already been happening for years: being the parent of a kid with one of the biggest, gentlest hearts I have ever witnessed, and having to fight — specifically, repeatedly, exhaustingly — for the basic premise that when he says he was hurt, he was actually hurt. Not fighting for leniency. Fighting for belief.

I don't know how to fully protect him from this yet. I know how to keep showing up, asking the same question every time until someone actually answers it: not "could he be misreading this," but "did you ask the other kids, separately, before you decided that?"

He deserves to be believed at the same rate as any kid who can deliver bad news in a calmer voice. He isn't yet. I'm not done fighting for the day he is.
