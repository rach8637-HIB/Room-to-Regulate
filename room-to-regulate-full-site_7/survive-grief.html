I Spent the Day Looking at Old Photos of Her Laughing

Grieving a version of motherhood I thought I'd have

4 min readEssay

There's a photo from when she was a baby — pure, uncomplicated delight, gums and all, the kind of laugh that takes over a kid's whole body. I know exactly which drawer of photos it lives in because I go back to it more than I'd admit out loud. Not for the memory. For the ache of it. For the version of this that I thought I was signing up for, back when I imagined what having a daughter would feel like, before I understood how much of most days would be spent in a body that thinks it's under attack.

I don't mean that as metaphor. I mean my heart rate. I mean the specific hum of being braced, low-grade and constant, for the next thing — the next meltdown, the next fight, the next moment where I have to decide in half a second whether to hold a boundary or hold her, and pick wrong more often than I want to admit. Fight or flight isn't supposed to be a parenting style. It's not supposed to be the baseline hum underneath an ordinary Tuesday. And most days, it is.

I grieve it in strange, unscheduled moments. Not dramatically. Just a quiet, private ache that shows up while I'm scrolling old photos on my phone, missing a version of this that I'm not sure ever fully existed the way I remember it, mourning the parts of motherhood I pictured — the ease, the softness, the version where I'm mostly calm and she's mostly regulated and neither of us is white-knuckling our way through an average afternoon.

I am allowed to mourn a version of this I didn't get, without that grief being proof that I caused the version I did get.

And right behind the grief, every single time, comes the other thing, quieter but somehow louder: maybe this is me. Maybe I'm the reason. Maybe a calmer parent would produce a calmer kid. Maybe the fight-or-flight isn't a response to what's actually happening in this house — maybe I'm the one generating the weather.

I don't have a tidy answer to that fear. I don't think it fully goes away. Some days I can look at it honestly and see how much of it isn't mine — her wiring, her nervous system, the parts of this that were true before I ever said a word to her. Other days the guilt wins, and I sit with old baby photos and grieve a version of both of us that maybe never would have existed no matter who was parenting her.

What I know for certain, on the days I can hold onto it: grief and guilt are not the same thing as truth. Both feelings are real. Only one of them is actually evidence.

Some days that distinction is everything. Other days, I'm just a woman looking at a photo of her baby laughing, missing something I can't fully name, and letting that be enough of the truth for today.
