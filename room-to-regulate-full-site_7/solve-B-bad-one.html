What to Say When Your Kid Says "I'm the Bad One"

4 min readFree script
The natural response is "no, you're not!" said fast, said with feeling. It doesn't land — it reads as "you didn't actually hear what I said."
Why the instinct to reassure usually backfires

The natural response to "I'm the bad one" is "no, you're not!" — said fast, said with feeling. It doesn't land. To a kid who's already decided this about themselves, a fast denial reads as if you didn't actually hear what I said — which confirms the isolation instead of interrupting it.

The fix: slow down and get specific, don't deny and move on
Instead of: "That's not true, you're not bad at all!"
Try: "Tell me more about that. What happened right before you started feeling that way?"

Let them answer. The goal in this first step isn't reassurance — it's finding out which specific moment got translated into "I'm bad," so you can address that moment instead of arguing with a feeling.

Instead of: "You're actually a really good kid."
Try: "What you did was [specific behavior]. That's a thing that happened, not a thing you are. Those are different."

This is the core move: separate the action from the identity, every single time, out loud, even when it feels repetitive. Kids build self-concept out of repetition. If the only repeated message is "bad things happened around you," that becomes the story.

Proving the story wrong with evidence, not argument
Try, unprompted, later: "Hey — I was thinking about how you noticed your sister was upset earlier and got her water without anyone asking. That was really thoughtful."

This does the quiet work of proving the "bad one" story wrong with evidence, on a day with no crisis attached to it — which is where it actually sinks in.

When it's your own dysregulation they're reacting to
Try: "That was a hard morning, and my voice got loud. That's on me, not on you. You didn't do anything to deserve that."

Naming your own part specifically and separately from theirs models that adults are allowed to be imperfect without it becoming a character flaw — the exact lesson you want them to internalize about themselves.

The takeaway

This won't undo a hard morning. It'll stop a hard morning from quietly becoming a kid's whole personality.
