She's the One Place That's Supposed to Feel Safe

2 min read Essay

The phone rings. It's my mom.

"Hey — guys, stop! Don't—" I say, half into the phone, half at the chaos behind me.

"This seems like a bad time," she says.

I say something snarky back. I'm tired. I'm hungry. There are dogs barking and children screaming, and none of it is her fault.

"Sorry I bothered you," she says. We hang up.

I just snapped at my mother like I'm ten years old — because she's supposed to be the one place that's still safe.

I feel like crying. Every part of the day has come at me like a cannonball I couldn't stop, and the guilt comes fast after, along with the self-doubt, cracking open old wounds I thought had closed.

My kids feel that way about me, too. I'm supposed to be their safe place. And on the days I can't be — because I'm running on no sleep, no food, and a to-do list that never ends — I know exactly what it costs them, because I felt it too, and still do.

I vow to walk in the door and be more understanding. I mean it, completely, for about four seconds.

Then I open the door. The scene behind it feels like a steam roller – the continuity of the chaos, and there's no time to be anything other than what's left of me.
